1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY & PROJECT PURPOSE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
What is CellGuard?
CellGuard is a battery diagnostic and prognostics platform designed for Lithium-ion 
batteries (specifically 18650 cylindrical cells). It uses machine learning to estimate 
a battery's internal health parameters—specifically Ohmic Resistance (Re) and 
Charge-Transfer Resistance (Rct)—directly from basic operational data (voltage, 
current, temperature, and cycle number) during normal battery discharge.

In Simple Words:
Normally, checking a battery's internal health requires stopping the vehicle, taking 
the battery to an advanced laboratory, and connecting it to an expensive spectrometer 
for hours. CellGuard replaces that expensive laboratory test with smart software. 
It watches how the voltage and temperature change during standard use, and immediately 
predicts the internal resistance with over 96% accuracy.

2. THE CORE ENGINEERING PROBLEM WE ARE SOLVING
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Why Do Batteries Age?
As a Lithium-ion battery charges and discharges over hundreds of cycles, physical 
and chemical degradation takes place inside the cell:
1. Electrolyte degradation & contact wear: The liquid electrolyte gradually loses 
   conductivity, and physical tab connections degrade. This increases Ohmic 
   Resistance (Re).
2. Solid Electrolyte Interphase (SEI) Layer Growth: Parasitic chemical reactions 
   between the lithium ions and the organic electrolyte create a resistive coating 
   on the graphite anode surface. As this coating thickens, it becomes harder and 
   harder for lithium ions to pass across the interface. This increases 
   Charge-Transfer Resistance (Rct).

Why Is This a Problem in Electric Vehicles (EVs) & Energy Storage Systems?
- Voltage Drop Under Load: According to Ohm's Law, when an electric vehicle accelerates 
  and draws high current (I), the internal resistance causes an immediate voltage drop:
      Delta V = I * (Re + Rct)
  Higher resistance causes the battery to hit the low-voltage cutoff limit prematurely, 
  stranding the vehicle even when chemical energy remains.
- Joule Heating & Fire Hazard: Power lost as waste heat inside the battery is:
      P_loss = I^2 * (Re + Rct)
  As Re and Rct increase, heat generation grows exponentially, placing huge stress 
  on the cooling system and risking thermal runaway.

The Real-World Hardware Dilemma:
The gold standard for measuring Re and Rct is Electrochemical Impedance Spectroscopy (EIS). 
However, EIS cannot be used in commercial EVs or active solar battery banks because:
1. It requires the battery to be in an idle rest state for hours at strict temperature 
   equilibrium (24 deg C).
2. It requires injecting complex sinusoidal AC currents from 0.1 Hz to 5,000 Hz.
3. The measurement hardware is bulky, fragile, expensive, and sensitive to automotive 
   electrical noise.

CellGuard's Solution:
CellGuard extracts 19 non-invasive physical features from the observable partial 
discharge window (from 4.0 V down to 3.4 V). A trained Extra Trees machine learning 
ensemble then maps these operational voltage and temperature signatures directly to 
Re and Rct in real time—without needing any EIS hardware.

3. WHICH MACHINE LEARNING MODELS ARE USED & WHY
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
We evaluated four different machine learning algorithms representing distinct 
mathematical paradigms:

1. Extra Trees Regressor (Primary Production Model - Winner)
   - Architecture: Extremely Randomized Trees Ensemble (150 trees).
   - How it works: Builds randomized decision trees. Cuts the feature space at 
     random orthogonal thresholds and averages the predictions.
   - Fit Accuracy (R^2): Re = 97.01%, Rct = 96.83%
   - Mean Absolute Error (MAE): Re = 1.13 mOhm, Rct = 1.28 mOhm
   - Why it wins: Handles non-linear electrochemical curves, is immune to sensor 
     noise, and trains rapidly.

2. XGBoost Regressor (Strong Alternative Engine)
   - Architecture: Gradient Boosted Decision Trees.
   - How it works: Iteratively builds sequential trees where each new tree corrects 
     the residual errors made by the previous trees.
   - Fit Accuracy (R^2): Re = 96.02%, Rct = 95.83%
   - Mean Absolute Error (MAE): Re = 1.24 mOhm, Rct = 1.47 mOhm

3. Random Forest Regressor (Robust Benchmark Model)
   - Architecture: Bagging Ensemble of Decision Trees (150 trees).
   - How it works: Builds decision trees using bootstrap data samples and finds 
     the optimal split per feature.
   - Fit Accuracy (R^2): Re = 95.68%, Rct = 95.41%
   - Mean Absolute Error (MAE): Re = 1.25 mOhm, Rct = 1.50 mOhm

4. Ridge Regression (Linear Baseline Model)
   - Architecture: L2-Regularized Linear Model.
   - How it works: Fits a linear hyperplane (y = w^T * x + b) with a penalty on 
     large weights to prevent overfitting.
   - Fit Accuracy (R^2): Re = 90.29%, Rct = 90.60%
   - Mean Absolute Error (MAE): Re = 2.28 mOhm, Rct = 2.44 mOhm
   - Why linear models fall behind: Battery degradation is non-linear; linear hyperplanes 
     cannot capture sudden acceleration in resistance growth.

4. INPUT FEATURES VS TARGET OUTPUTS (ZERO DATA LEAKAGE GUARANTEE)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
In academic machine learning, data leakage is a critical failure mode (e.g., using 
the target variable or future information as an input). CellGuard strictly guarantees 
zero data leakage.

Input Features (X - Exactly 19 Parameters):
These are parameters an onboard Battery Management System (BMS) can measure directly:
1. Voltage Crossing Times (5 features): Time taken to discharge between voltage 
   thresholds: T_40_38 (4.0V to 3.8V), T_38_36, T_36_34, T_40_36, T_38_34.
2. Partial Discharge Capacity (1 feature): Q_40_36 (Coulomb count between 4.0V and 3.6V).
3. Transient Voltage Samples (4 features): Terminal voltage recorded at specific 
   timestamps: V_at_60s, V_at_300s, V_at_600s, V_at_1200s.
4. Voltage Curve Dynamics (3 features): Initial voltage V_start, mean early voltage 
   V_mean_first600s, and mid-discharge slope V_slope_mid.
5. Temperature Dynamics (4 features): Starting temperature T_start, peak temperature 
   T_max, total temperature rise T_rise, and average temperature T_mean.
6. Load Step Resistance Proxy (1 feature): R_dc_proxy (Delta V / Delta I during the 
   initial load step).
7. Aging Index (1 feature): disc_idx (Discharge cycle number).

Target Outputs (y):
- Ohmic Resistance (Re): Predicted in Ohms and displayed in milliohms (mOhm).
- Charge-Transfer Resistance (Rct): Predicted in Ohms and displayed in milliohms (mOhm).

IMPORTANT VIVA POINT:
Neither Re nor Rct is EVER present in the input feature matrix X. The models learn 
the mapping solely from voltage, temperature, and cycle time.

5. DATASET & NUMERICAL PERFORMANCE BREAKDOWN
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The NASA Battery Aging Dataset:
- Source: NASA Ames Prognostics Center of Excellence (PCoE).
- Core Benchmark Cells: B0005, B0006, B0007, B0018.
  - Type: 18650 cylindrical Lithium-ion cells (LiCoO2 chemistry).
  - Rated Nominal Capacity: 2.0 Ah.
  - Test Conditions: Constant Current (CC) discharge at 2.0 A (1C rate) down to 
    2.7V, 2.5V, 2.2V, and 2.5V respectively, maintained in a thermal chamber at 24 deg C.
- Expanded Scope: 34 total NASA battery cells across the archive.

Data Cleaning & Outlier Removal:
In NASA's raw dataset, internal resistance was extracted using nonlinear circle-fitting 
in MATLAB. In 7 cycles out of 2,058, the MATLAB optimization routine failed to converge, 
recording impossible non-physical values (e.g. -9.6 * 10^14 Ohm).
- CellGuard filters data to physical boundaries:
  - 0.010 Ohm <= Re <= 0.300 Ohm (10 mOhm to 300 mOhm)
  - 0.020 Ohm <= Rct <= 0.450 Ohm (20 mOhm to 450 mOhm)
- This leaves 530 verified paired cycles across the 4 core benchmark cells and 
  2,051 verified cycles across all cells.

Performance Scores:
- Ohmic Resistance (Re):
  - Coefficient of Determination (R^2): 97.01%
  - Mean Absolute Error (MAE): 1.13 mOhm (0.00113 Ohm)
  - Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE): 1.66 mOhm
  - Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE): 1.97%
- Charge-Transfer Resistance (Rct):
  - Coefficient of Determination (R^2): 96.83%
  - Mean Absolute Error (MAE): 1.28 mOhm (0.00128 Ohm)
  - Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE): 1.90 mOhm
  - Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE): 1.55%

Both models significantly exceed the university requirement of R^2 > 80%.

6. TRAINING & TESTING SPLIT: METHODOLOGY & DEEP EXPLANATION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Why a Standard Random 80/20 Train-Test Split is FLAWED for Batteries:
In typical machine learning projects (like image classification or housing prices), 
data points are independent. You can randomly shuffle the dataset into 80% train 
and 20% test.

In battery aging, random splitting is a critical methodological error known as 
TEMPORAL DATA LEAKAGE:
- Cycle 41 and Cycle 42 of cell B0005 have virtually identical chemistry, internal 
  resistance, and thermal history.
- If Cycle 41 is placed in the training set and Cycle 42 in the test set, the model 
  does not actually learn physics—it simply memorizes adjacent data points. This 
  yields artificially high, fake scores (e.g. 99.9%) that fail completely when applied 
  to a new battery in the real world.

The Two Validation Strategies Used in CellGuard:

Strategy A: 5-Fold Stratified Group Cross-Validation (Operational Accuracy)
- The dataset is split into 5 distinct folds where whole groups of cycles are held out.
- Each cycle is predicted strictly when it is in the holdout fold.
- Why this was chosen: Evaluates how accurately the model tracks resistance evolution 
  over a cell's lifecycle when trained on representative operating conditions.
- Result: R^2 = 97.01% for Re, 96.83% for Rct.

Strategy B: Leave-One-Cell-Out (LOCO) 4-Fold Cross-Validation (Cross-Cell Generalization)
- In each fold, the model is trained exclusively on three cells (e.g., B0005, B0006, 
  B0007) and tested on the fourth completely unseen cell (B0018).
- Why this was chosen: Proves whether the algorithm can generalize to a brand-new 
  battery that came off the assembly line.
- The Physical Baseline Offset Discovery:
  - Real commercial 18650 cells have manufacturing tolerances: their initial fresh 
    resistance varies by +/- 10 mOhm to 15 mOhm (e.g., B0005 starts at 55 mOhm, 
    while B0018 starts at 38 mOhm).
  - When evaluating zero-shot on an unseen cell, this constant 15 mOhm vertical offset 
    reduces raw uncalibrated R^2.
  - However, when the BMS takes one single calibration measurement at Cycle 1, 
    the model predicts the degradation curve (Delta R) with R^2 > 92% across all 
    unseen cells!

7. ELECTRICAL ENGINEERING (EEE) CONCEPTS EXPLAINED SIMPLY
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1. The 1st-Order Thevenin Equivalent Circuit Model (ECM):
In battery modeling, the complex electrochemistry is represented by an electrical circuit:
- OCV (Open Circuit Voltage): The ideal equilibrium voltage of the cell when no 
  current is flowing.
- Re approx R0 (Ohmic Resistance): Resistor in series representing the electrolyte 
  and current collector bulk resistance. Responds instantaneously (t = 0+).
- Rct approx R1 (Charge-Transfer Resistance): Resistor in parallel with capacitor C1 
  representing the electrode-electrolyte interface kinetics.
- C1 (Double-Layer Capacitance): Models the capacitive charge buildup at the electrode surface.
- Terminal Voltage Equation:
      V_terminal(t) = OCV - I(t) * Re - V_RC(t)
      d V_RC(t) / dt = -V_RC(t) / (Rct * C1) + I(t) / C1

2. Terminal Voltage Drop (Delta V):
When a vehicle steps on the accelerator (current demand I):
      Delta V_total = I * (Re + Rct)
For example, at a discharge current of I = 10 A:
- A fresh cell with R_int = 100 mOhm drops by Delta V = 10 * 0.100 = 1.0 V.
- An aged cell with R_int = 220 mOhm drops by Delta V = 10 * 0.220 = 2.2 V.
This large voltage drop causes the BMS to trigger premature low-voltage cutoff!

3. Joule Heating & Power Losses (P_loss):
Internal power lost as pure heat is given by Joule's Law:
      P_loss = I^2 * (Re + Rct)
At I = 15 A:
- Fresh cell (0.100 Ohm): P_loss = (15)^2 * 0.100 = 22.5 W of heat.
- Aged cell (0.220 Ohm): P_loss = (15)^2 * 0.220 = 49.5 W of heat (> 2.2x more heat!).
This explains why aging batteries get hot much faster and can trigger thermal alarms.

4. Why Rct Grows Much Faster Than Re:
- Re growth: Bulk electrolyte volume and current collectors degrade slowly 
  (~20% to 40% growth over battery life).
- Rct growth: Parasitic reactions continuously deposit passivating compounds 
  (Li2CO3, LiF) onto the anode, creating a thick Solid Electrolyte Interphase (SEI) 
  barrier. As a result, Rct typically grows by 100% to 250%.
- Conclusion: Rct is the primary indicator of interfacial electrochemical degradation.

8. VIVA VOCE EXAMINER QUESTIONS & DIRECT ANSWERS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Q1: What is the main objective of your project?
Answer: The main objective is to estimate the internal Ohmic Resistance (Re) and 
Charge-Transfer Resistance (Rct) of Lithium-ion batteries using machine learning 
applied to observable partial discharge curves, eliminating the need for expensive, 
offline Electrochemical Impedance Spectroscopy (EIS) hardware.

Q2: Did you use Re or Rct as input features to predict each other or capacity?
Answer: Absolutely not. That would be data leakage. The inputs are strictly 19 
observable electrical and thermal parameters extracted from the first portion of the 
discharge curve (voltage crossing times, load-step voltage drop proxy, temperatures, 
and cycle number). Re and Rct are strictly target outputs.

Q3: Why did you choose Extra Trees as your primary algorithm?
Answer: Extra Trees achieved the highest performance with an R^2 of 97.01% for Re 
and 96.83% for Rct, with an average error of only ~1.1 to 1.3 mOhm. Tree ensembles 
partition the feature space orthogonally, capturing the non-linear relationship between 
terminal voltage overpotentials and internal resistance that linear models cannot.

Q4: Why did Ridge Regression achieve lower performance than tree ensembles?
Answer: Ridge Regression is a linear model (y = w^T * x + b). Electrochemical 
degradation is non-linear; as resistance grows, the voltage drop and temperature rise 
accelerate non-linearly. A linear hyperplane cannot capture threshold crossings or 
interaction terms between temperature and load step drop.

Q5: Why did you not use a standard random 80/20 train/test split?
Answer: Random train/test splitting causes temporal data leakage in battery cycling. 
Adjacent cycles from the same physical battery share almost identical chemical 
degradation and temperature history. Random splitting places Cycle 41 in training 
and Cycle 42 in testing, leading to memorization rather than generalization. Instead, 
we used 5-Fold Stratified Group Cross-Validation and Leave-One-Cell-Out (LOCO) 
cross-validation.

Q6: What is the physical meaning of Re and Rct in an electric vehicle battery?
Answer: Re is the Ohmic bulk resistance of the electrolyte, separator, and metallic 
contacts. Rct is the charge-transfer resistance across the electrode-electrolyte 
interface, reflecting SEI layer growth. Together, they dictate the terminal voltage 
drop (Delta V = I*(Re + Rct)) and internal heat dissipation (P_loss = I^2*(Re + Rct)).

Q7: How does your project relate to the Thevenin Equivalent Circuit?
Answer: In a 1st-order Thevenin model (OCV - R0 - R1 || C1), our predicted Re corresponds 
directly to the instantaneous series resistance R0, and Rct corresponds to the 
polarization resistance R1.
